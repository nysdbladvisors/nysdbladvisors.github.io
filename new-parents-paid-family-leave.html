FOR NEW YORK NEW PARENTS

Take Paid Time Off to Bond With Your New Baby in New York

Having a baby, adopting, or welcoming a foster child? New York Paid Family Leave can pay up to 67% of your weekly wages for up to 12 weeks, and your job is protected. Birth parents may also qualify for disability benefits during recovery.

Who Can Take Leave to Bond With a Child

Each qualifying parent can take their own paid leave to bond with a new child.

Birth parent
Spouse or domestic partner
Adoptive parent
Foster parent

You don’t need to be married. A domestic partner doesn’t need legal registration, but must be at least 18 and depend on you for support. Bonding leave must be taken within the first 12 months after the birth, adoption, or foster placement. Not sure where you fit? Ask us for free.

Estimate Your Benefit

PFL pays 67% of your average weekly wage, up to the weekly maximum. Your average weekly wage is generally the average of your last eight weeks of pay before leave. Your insurance carrier decides the actual amount, and benefits are taxable.

Birth Parents: Disability First, Then Family Leave

If you’re giving birth, two different benefits can apply, one after the other.

1. Disability (DBL) for recovery

DBL can pay part of your wages while you recover from childbirth, generally up to 6 weeks after delivery, or 8 weeks after a C-section. It pays 50% of your average weekly wage, up to $170 a week. Pregnancy complications before delivery may also qualify, with your doctor’s support.

2. Paid Family Leave to bond

After recovery, PFL can pay while you bond with your baby, up to 12 weeks. PFL can’t start before the baby is born, and DBL and PFL can’t be collected for the same days. Together they’re limited to 26 weeks in a 52-week period.

Planning ahead helps. Tell your employer about your plans as early as you can, ideally at least 30 days before leave begins, and talk to your doctor about your expected recovery dates. If you’re not the birth parent, PFL is the benefit that applies to you. New to these programs? Read FMLA vs. PFL vs. DBL: What’s the Difference?

Do You Qualify?

In general, you qualify for PFL if you work for a covered New York employer and meet one of these:

Full-time employees

You’ve worked for your employer for 26 consecutive weeks, working 20 or more hours a week.

Part-time employees

You’ve worked 175 days in total, working fewer than 20 hours a week.

Rules differ for some public employees, contractors, and people who work outside New York. Your insurance carrier makes the final decision. A free questionnaire review can tell you where you stand. Start here.

Documents You’ll Need

You send proof of your relationship to the child with your Bonding Certification (Form PFL-2). What you need depends on your situation. Send copies unless the state asks for an original.

Birth of a child

Birth parent, one of:

  • A health care provider’s certification of pregnancy (with your name and expected due date)
  • A health care provider’s certification of birth (with your name and the child’s date of birth)
  • The child’s birth certificate

Other parent, one of:

  • A birth certificate naming you
  • A Voluntary Acknowledgment of Parentage
  • A court order of filiation
  • The birth parent’s documents plus a marriage certificate, certificate of civil union, or evidence of domestic partnership

Adoption

  • Court documents finalizing the adoption, or documentation in furtherance of the adoption
  • If you’re the second parent and aren’t named in the legal documents, proof of your relationship to the parent who is, such as a marriage or domestic partner document

PFL can sometimes begin before the adoption is finalized, for example when you need to appear in court or travel to complete it. Include documentation showing the leave is needed.

Foster placement

  • A foster care placement letter (or anticipated placement) from your county or city department of social services or an authorized voluntary foster care agency
  • If you’re the second parent and aren’t named in the letter, proof of your relationship to the parent who is

If none of these apply, other documentation of your parental relationship may be accepted. Not sure what to send? Ask us for free.

How Applying Works

Applying for bonding leave involves you, your employer, and the insurance carrier.

You

  • Notify your employer 30 days ahead if you can
  • Complete Part A of the Request for Paid Family Leave (Form PFL-1) and give a copy to your employer
  • Complete the Bonding Certification (Form PFL-2) and attach your documents
  • Mail or fax both forms to the carrier

Your employer

  • Completes Part B of Form PFL-1
  • Must return it to you within 3 business days
  • If there’s a delay, you don’t have to wait. Send your request to the carrier without it

The insurance carrier

  • Pays or denies the claim, in most cases within 18 calendar days of receiving your complete request or your first day of leave, whichever is later
  • If denied, must tell you why and how to request arbitration
Don’t miss the 30-day deadline. Your completed request must reach the insurance carrier within 30 days after your leave starts, or you could lose benefits. Until the carrier approves your request, you’re not considered to be on Paid Family Leave, and your employer decides how to treat the time off.

New Parent Questions

Quick answers about taking Paid Family Leave to bond with a new child.

Can both parents take Paid Family Leave?

Yes. Each parent who qualifies can take up to 12 weeks of PFL to bond with the child. This includes a spouse, domestic partner, adoptive parent, or foster parent.

When can I start PFL after my baby is born?

PFL for bonding can’t start before the child is born. For a birth parent, disability (DBL) usually covers the recovery period first, and PFL can follow. Bonding leave also has to be taken within the first 12 months after the birth, adoption, or placement.

Do I need a doctor’s note to bond with my baby?

You don’t need a medical certification of a health condition, since no one is claiming an illness. Instead, you complete the Bonding Certification (Form PFL-2) and attach proof of the relationship, such as a birth certificate or a health care provider’s certification of birth. Adoptive and foster parents attach court or placement documents. See the list above.

Can I apply before the baby is born?

Possibly. The state forms allow you to submit in advance (“pre-submitting”) when you’re planning ahead, for example with a health care provider’s certification of pregnancy that includes the due date. If the carrier permits it, they’ll tell you how to send the birth documentation later. PFL still can’t start before the baby is born.

Can I take leave a few days at a time?

Yes. You can take PFL all at once or in separate full days, up to 12 weeks in a 52-week period. Leave is counted in full-day increments, so partial days don’t work.

What if my due date is near the end of the year?

The maximum weekly benefit generally depends on the year your leave starts. For leave starting in 2026 the maximum is $1,228.53 a week, and for leave starting in 2027 it’s $1,287.91. Our calculator lets you pick the year.

Will I get my full paycheck?

No. PFL pays 67% of your average weekly wage, up to the weekly maximum. Benefits are also taxable. Some employers let you combine PFL with accrued paid time off, but that’s your employer’s policy, so ask HR.

Will my job be protected?

Yes. While you’re on approved PFL, your job is protected and your health insurance continues. You’re generally entitled to return to the same or a comparable position.

How much notice do I need to give my employer?

If you know about the leave ahead of time, you should give at least 30 days’ notice. If it isn’t foreseeable, notify your employer as soon as you can.

What if I’ve been denied?

The carrier must tell you why and explain how to request arbitration. We can walk you through your options for free.

Do I have to use your service?

No. You can apply on your own for free using the official forms. If you’d like help, we’re here, and the first conversation is always free.
